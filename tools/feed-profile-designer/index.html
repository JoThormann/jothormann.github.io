The model

F is the pump setpoint in mL/min  →  Fg = F · 0.06 · cfeed (g/h)  ·  uptake U = min(Fg + pool, qS,max·X·V)  ·  Uox = min(U, qS,crit·X·V), Uof = U − Uox  ·  d(XV)/dt = Y (Uox − msXV) + Yof Uof  ·  OUR = (O₂/g · Uox + O₂of · Uof) / V ≤ ceiling

  • One controlled variable. Every phase sets volumetric flow, because that is what a pump actually takes. A fixed-k exponential is an open-loop programme: the pump follows the curve whether or not the culture does. The demand-matched shape is the exception — it recomputes F each step from current biomass, so μ is held rather than assumed.
  • Substrate-limited where it can be. Below qS,crit every gram fed is respired on arrival, so the feed — not the strain — sets μ. Above it the extra uptake overflows: biomass at the low yield Yof, the organism's product (acetate for E. coli, ethanol for yeast), and only the oxygen the electron balance still needs. Uptake itself stops at qS,max, chosen so growth on excess substrate is exactly μmax; anything fed faster stays in the broth as residual substrate and is taken up later. The dotted line in panel D is the μ the feed alone would give.
  • The OUR ceiling is a hard limit. If a plan needs more oxygen than the vessel can transfer, OUR is held at the ceiling, uptake is scaled back to fit, and the plan is flagged as an error — on the reactor that moment is a DO crash.
  • Oxygen couples to carbon stoichiometrically, so an OUR ceiling is identical to a ceiling on the specific feed rate in g substrate/min/L — the same number whatever the feed concentration.
  • Dilution split. Absolute demand grows at μ. Volume grows at D = (dV/dt)/V, so concentration only grows at μ − D. A dilute feed spends a large share of the exponential on water.

The batch phase

  • The batch is modelled as unrestricted exponential growth at μmax for the batch temperature: t_batch = lag + ln(X_end / X_inoc) / μmax, with X_end = X_inoc + Y · S₀. Feed start is where the substrate runs out, which is the moment the DO trace spikes.
  • Fixing the length overrides the prediction but not the physics: if the fixed length is shorter than the batch needs, the leftover substrate is reported, because the feed then starts into a culture that is not yet carbon-limited and the substrate-limited assumption does not hold from t = 0.
  • Oxygen uptake during the batch comes from the same stoichiometry as the feed phase, so the predicted OUR peak at exhaustion is directly comparable to what the off-gas will show.

Temperature

  • Each phase carries a target, and the vessel moves toward it at the ramp rate you set rather than stepping instantly. Maintenance is scaled with Q10 = 2 and μmax by a Ratkowsky square-root law, both recalculated at every step from the temperature the vessel is actually at.
  • That means μmax is a curve, not a constant: a μ setpoint chosen at batch temperature can end up above μmax once the vessel has cooled. The growth panel draws μmax as a moving line so you can see it happen.

Where the oxygen number comes from

  • O₂ per gram is derived, not typed: an electron balance over biomass, substrate and oxygen. With biomass of degree of reduction γX and substrate γS, mmol O2/g = 1000 (γS − γX·Y·M_S/M_X) / (4·M_S). That keeps the biomass curve and the oxygen curve describing the same organism, which two independent inputs do not.
  • If you have a measured off-gas value, Expert mode lets you enter it and shows the yield that measurement implies. When that disagrees with the yield you entered, the two curves are describing different organisms and one of the numbers is wrong. Maintenance cannot explain a measurement that comes in below the balance — it pushes oxygen demand up. Secreted product can: a reduced product carries electrons that would otherwise have gone to oxygen.

How the organism and the oxygen supply change the answer

  • The organism sets the balance. Biomass composition fixes γX and the C-mol mass, so the same yield implies different oxygen demand for E. coli (CH1.77O0.49N0.24) than for yeast (CH1.76O0.56N0.17). It also sets μmax, the maintenance rate and the temperature response.
  • Aerobic is the case the OUR ceiling is for. Oxygen transfer is finite, so the exponential eventually outruns it and the feed has to stop climbing. With a dilute feed, volume runs out first instead.
  • Anaerobic removes the oxygen ceiling entirely — and with it most of the yield. Around 0.10 g/g instead of 0.50 means roughly five times the substrate and five times the feed volume for the same biomass, so a run that was oxygen-bound becomes volume-bound. The electrons that no longer go to oxygen leave in a reduced product: for yeast on glucose, about 0.45 g ethanol per g, against the 0.511 theoretical maximum.
  • Overflow is a carbon limit, not an oxygen one. Crabtree-positive yeast makes ethanol above roughly 0.25 1/h even under full aeration, and E. coli makes acetate above its critical uptake rate. Feeding below qS,crit is the entire reason exponential fed-batch exists. Past it the tool splits the flux (see above), so DCW and OUR both come out lower than a single-yield model would say. The overflow product is not taken back up here, which makes the product figure an upper bound; and the batch keeps its simple single-yield picture, on the assumption that batch overflow is re-consumed before the DO spike.
  • Weighed form. Glucose is usually weighed as the monohydrate; pick it under Carbon source and the strength you type is what went on the balance, while every g/L shown is anhydrous glucose (600 g monohydrate per kg of solution is about 685 g/L of glucose, not 773).
  • Not modelled: recombinant product formation drawing on the same carbon, re-uptake of overflow product, inhibition by it, oxygen transfer falling as fill height rises, evaporation, and sampling losses. Treat the endgame numbers as an upper bound.
